# λφ LambdaPhi Foundation

**Making software, challenging the status quo.**

The **LambdaPhi Foundation** is an umbrella for projects exploring formal logic, programming languages, low-level systems, and a bit of *cursed engineering*.

We are interested in computing at the level where abstractions become assumptions, and assumptions become things worth questioning.

This is not a corporation, a product studio, or an attempt to manufacture the next industry standard. It is a space for experimentation: building things, breaking things, and discovering what happens when we approach familiar problems from unfamiliar directions.

### --> Software & Learning

We build software not merely to provide convenient solutions, but to understand the systems those solutions are built upon.

Modern software often places layer after layer between the programmer and the machine. Those layers are useful, but they can also make the underlying system invisible. We are interested in pulling some of those layers apart.

Our approach is heavily influenced by **learning through construction**. Sometimes the best way to understand a system is to build the part that a library normally hides from you.

We would rather build something the hard way once and understand every part of it than permanently depend on an abstraction we cannot explain.

The goal is not to reject abstraction.

**The goal is to know what is underneath it.**

### --> The Iconoclastic Approach

At the core of LambdaPhi is **conceptual iconoclasm**.

We question the assumptions that become invisible when a particular design has been used for long enough.

An industry standard is not necessarily a universal law. A conventional architecture is not necessarily the only architecture. A familiar abstraction is not necessarily the correct abstraction for every problem.

So we experiment.

We design alternative computational models.  
We build unconventional languages.  
We replace familiar abstractions with strange ones.  
We pursue ideas that may be impractical simply to discover *why* they are impractical.

Sometimes this means ignoring established workflows. Sometimes it means deliberately choosing the harder implementation. Sometimes it means making something gloriously cursed and iterating until the machine finally tells us where the idea breaks.

We are not primarily interested in producing the most polished software possible.

**We are interested in discovering what software can become when its assumptions are put on trial.**

### --> Recovering Ideas

Not every abandoned idea was a bad idea.

Some ideas disappear because the problem they were designed to solve disappears. Others are overtaken by newer techniques, become impractical under different constraints, or simply lose the attention of the people developing them.

We are interested in what remains useful inside those ideas.

By exploring unconventional, forgotten, or abandoned approaches, we may uncover mechanisms and ways of thinking that are valuable far beyond their original context. An architectural idea from an old machine might inspire a new programming model. A forgotten language feature might suggest a different approach to modern software. A technique that failed in one environment might become useful when its assumptions are changed.

We therefore treat old and unconventional ideas less as historical artifacts and more as **raw material**.

We do not seek to resurrect the past exactly as it was.

**We want to take its useful fragments, recompile them for new problems, and see what they become.**

### --> Flagship

Our flagship project is **MORAM**, a deque-based instruction set architecture exploring an alternative foundation for abstract machine design.

MORAM is an example of the kind of question LambdaPhi exists to ask:

> **What if the machine didn't have to look the way we normally expect a machine to look?**

Future projects may explore equally unconventional ideas across programming languages, operating systems, compilers, runtimes, machine architectures, and software licensing.

### --> Philosophy

LambdaPhi does not promise that every experiment will succeed.

Failure is useful.

A strange architecture that turns out to be terrible can teach us more than another implementation of an architecture nobody bothered to question.

Build it.  
Break it.  
Understand why.

**Recover what is discarded.  
Recompile what remains useful.**
